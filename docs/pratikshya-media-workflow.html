Media Workflow From image upload to product, review, marketing and storefront.

“Every image follows a controlled journey before it reaches the customer-facing storefront.”

This guide explains — for anyone, even someone who has never seen this project — what happens to a photograph inside Pratikshya Fashion: what happens, why it happens, who does it, what the system checks, what happens next, what can go wrong, and how it gets resolved. Every step, status and rule below was verified in the actual codebase. Nothing is invented; anything missing is marked Not Implemented.

Foundations

What is “media”?

A media asset is simply an image or visual file used by Pratikshya Fashion — a saree photograph, a kids-wear plate, a homepage banner. Before any image can appear in front of a customer, the system records it, names it, classifies it, and a human reviews it.

In plain words: think of media as a photograph with a passport. The passport says what the photo is, which product (if any) it belongs to, and whether it has been approved for travel to the storefront.
Product Media

Photography of a real product

Owned by exactly one product. Used for:

  • ▸ Product cards (the grid tiles customers browse)
  • ▸ Product detail pages & galleries
  • ▸ Front view · Side view · Back view · Detail view
  • ▸ Hover images (the second photo shown on mouse-over)
≠
Marketing Media

Editorial & promotional artwork

Owned by a marketing placement, never by a product. Used for:

  • ▸ Homepage hero carousel
  • ▸ Banners & sale campaigns
  • ▸ Editorial storytelling plates
  • ▸ Lookbook & collection covers
  • ▸ Homepage promotional sections

PRODUCT MEDIA ≠ MARKETING MEDIA. A record physically cannot be both at once: assigning media to a marketing placement clears its product ownership, and vice-versa. Implemented

The Journey

Master media flow — twelve steps, click each one

Every image travels these twelve stations. Click any tile to see what happens, who is responsible, what goes in, what comes out, the rules the system enforces, and what can go wrong.

Beginner tip: steps 1–5 are automatic (blue — the system does them). Steps 6–11 involve people (yellow — review; green — approval). Nothing skips a station.
Step 01 · 02 in depth

One library. One register. One identity per image.

Step 1 — Media enters the library Implemented

Every commercial image lives under one canonical media root:

public/library/ ← the single home for all photography (181 ingested assets)

Why one root? The system deliberately never searches random public folders. If images were scattered everywhere, no one could say which photo is official, reviewed, or owned by which product. One root means one truth: “all commercial media flows through the central media architecture.” A build script (npm run media:optimize) optimises files (80.2% size reduction on record) and writes the ingestion manifest. Admins and employees can also add media at runtime through the upload pages — with one honest caveat: runtime uploads are browser-preview only in the current demo (no server exists yet). Step 03 in depth

The filename is the truth

Pratikshya Fashion does not guess what a photo shows. The filename convention carries the facts, and a deterministic parser reads them. Same input, same answer, every time. Implemented

women-saree-001-front.webp └──────┬──────┘ └─┬─┘ │ │ women-saree-001 front ↓ ↓ groupKey view (which product-set) (which angle) women-saree-001-back.webp → same groupKey · different view therefore: ONE PRODUCT ├── FRONT └── BACK
In plain words: the part before the view word is the product-set’s name tag (groupKey); the last word says which angle it is. Two files with the same tag are two photos of the same physical garment.

Full convention: [department]-[category]-[style]-[number]-[view].webp. A file with no view word (like kids-001.webp) is a standalone — one photo, one product, no hover alternate.

Technical implementation
parser: parseMediaFilename(), 17 recognised view suffixes (longest first) — src/services/media/mediaNaming.js
view ordering: getViewOrderScore() (front 0 · side 1 · back 3 · detail 4 …)

The Most Important Rule

SIMILAR DOES NOT MEAN SAME PRODUCT

Two photographs may look almost identical — same colour, same model, same background. That proves nothing. Two similar-looking kids outfits can be two different physical products. The system therefore relies only on deterministic signals: the groupKey, the filename convention, explicit ownership in the register, and human review. Visual similarity is treated as a question for a human, never as an answer. The system will refuse to automatically merge visually similar products — and for the 21 confirmed Kids products (KID-001 … KID-021) it refuses even when a human tries: “Similar is not the same — they cannot be grouped as one product.”

Step 05 in depth

Grouping — one garment, many angles

Grouping collects the photos that show the same physical product from different angles. It is decided purely by the filename. Implemented

One group → one product
saree-001-front.webp saree-001-side.webp saree-001-back.webp ↓ GROUP saree-001 ↓ ONE PRODUCT (3 views)

Same groupKey ⇒ same garment. The group becomes one product with a front, side and back.

Never merged automatically
saree-001-front.webp saree-002-front.webp ↓ TWO GROUPS saree-001 · saree-002 ↓ TWO PRODUCTS — always

001 and 002 are different numbers, not view words. Different groupKeys are never auto-merged — only an explicit human “same product” decision can join them (and never for confirmed Kids).

Technical implementation
buildMediaGroups() — src/services/media/mediaGroups.js · one group = files whose base matches after removing the view suffix
primary = front-first (group.primary) · standalone vs multi-view: isStandalone / isGrouped
Step 06 in depth

Product mapping — where does this image belong?

After grouping, the system decides the destination of the media: an existing product, a brand-new product draft, the marketing world, or the review queue. Implemented

MEDIA (a group of 1+ files) │ Is it marketing / house artwork? ├── YES ──▶ MARKETING MEDIA │ (never becomes a product — excluded from discovery) └── NO │ Does product ownership already exist? (register productId → product claim → source group → canonical map) ├── YES ──▶ EXISTING PRODUCT (media attaches / already attached) └── NO │ Ingestion flags? duplicates? uncertainty? ├── YES ──▶ REVIEW QUEUE (a human decides) └── NO ──▶ CREATE / RECONCILE PRODUCT DRAFT (one draft per group · permanent Product ID · never auto-published)
In plain words: the system first checks “is this an advert, not a product photo?” Then “do we already know whose photo this is?” Only when the answer is no-and-no does it create a new draft product — and even then, a human must finish and approve it before customers ever see it.

The Ownership Law

Every photograph belongs to exactly one product

Ownership is written on the media record itself (its register ownership). A product may also claim media — and when a claim disagrees with the register, that is a conflict, reported loudly and never resolved silently. Implemented

Correct
Product KID-001 ├── kids-001-front.webp ✓ owned └── kids-001-back.webp ✓ owned
Never allowed
Product KID-001 └── saree-003-back.webp ✗ another product's photo
Cross-product media

BLOCKED. A product can never publish while it shows another product’s photograph. Card assembly drops foreign items outright and flags the set CROSS_PRODUCT_REFERENCE.

Duplicate ownership

REVIEW. The same file owned by two products is detected and counted in the workflow metrics; a human resolves it through the review actions. (Detection: implemented · guided auto-repair: not implemented.)

Invalid ownership

REVIEW. Media pointing at a product ID that no longer exists is reported as orphaned in the metrics. No automatic repair — a human re-maps it.

Technical implementation
single-owner check: validateMediaAssignment() → MEDIA_ALREADY_ASSIGNED — src/services/productWorkflow.js
claims vs register: resolveProductMediaClaims() — src/services/media/productMediaSet.js
cross-product drop: isProductOwnedMedia() / assembleProductMediaSet() · Kids cross checks: kidsMediaOwnershipIssues()
duplicate/orphan detection: getWorkflowMetrics().media.duplicateOwnership / orphaned

Human Decisions

The five review decisions

When media ownership is contested — most famously in the Kids reconciliation, where a draft’s photo is owned by an existing published product — an admin picks one of five actions. Each is a single, logged, deliberate act. Implemented

Keep Existing

When to use
The current owner is right; the new draft is redundant.
What it does
Clears the draft’s media claims and archives the draft.
What changes
Media stays with its current product; the draft leaves the queue.
Who can do it
Admin only.
What happens next
The existing product continues unchanged.

Transfer

When to use
The photo genuinely belongs to the new (e.g. KID) product.
What it does
Moves ownership with explicit confirmation; strips the previous owner’s stale references and flags it MEDIA_OWNERSHIP_MOVED; a published kids owner left with no media is archived.
What changes
The media record’s productId; both product records; the activity diary.
Who can do it
Admin only.
What happens next
Conflict flags are removed; the draft can proceed.

Merge

When to use
Draft and owner are the same real product; the draft holds better data.
What it does
Copies the draft’s real name/price/subcategory/descriptions onto the single owning product, then archives the draft.
What changes
The owner’s commercial fields; the draft’s status.
Who can do it
Admin only (requires exactly one owner).
What happens next
One product remains, enriched.

Create Separate Product

When to use
They only look similar — they are different physical products.
What it does
The draft keeps its identity but releases the contested media and gains the NEEDS_MEDIA flag.
What changes
Draft claims cleared; new photography required before publish.
Who can do it
Admin only.
What happens next
Draft waits for its own media.

Review Later

When to use
The answer isn’t clear yet; defer honestly.
What it does
Records CONFLICT_REVIEW_LATER; the case stays open in the queue.
What changes
Nothing else — and that is the point.
Who can do it
Admin only.
What happens next
Publishing stays blocked until a real decision is made. Deferral never unlocks anything.

Why Transfer needs confirmation

Reassigning media without confirmation is refused by the repository itself — the call simply returns nothing unless the caller passes an explicit confirm flag. This makes silent theft of another product’s photograph impossible: every transfer is a conscious human act, recorded in the activity diary with actor, source and destination.

Technical implementation
actions: KIDS_CONFLICT_ACTIONS / reconcileKidsConflict() — src/services/productWorkflow.js
confirmation gate: assignToProduct(…, {confirmReassign:true}) · one-door transfer: transferMediaOwnership()
group decisions (SAME_PRODUCT / SEPARATE_PRODUCTS / REVIEW_LATER): decideProductGroup() + src/services/media/productMediaGroups.js

The Publishing Gate — part one

Review flags: honest sticky notes on a draft

A flag says “a human must look at this”. Flags are review signals — never a second status system. While a blocking flag stands, the product cannot publish. Implemented

NAME REVIEW REQUIRED

The name is a placeholder (“Uncatalogued Saree · SAR-005”) or reads like another department’s product. Fix: type a real product name — the flag is then recognised as satisfied.

PRICE REVIEW REQUIRED

The price is still zero because the system refused to guess one. Fix: enter a real selling price greater than zero.

TAXONOMY REVIEW REQUIRED

Category was inferred from the filename, or the subcategory is missing. Fix: choose the correct category and subcategory from the existing taxonomy.

GROUPING REVIEW REQUIRED

Ingestion flagged the media group (possible duplicate, unmapped, needs-review). Fix: an admin answers the identity question in Group Review — same product or separate products. stored as GROUP_REVIEW_REQUIRED

Five more blocking flags exist for media problems — VARIANT_REVIEW_REQUIRED, NEEDS_MEDIA, MEDIA_OWNERSHIP_REVIEW, CONFLICT_UNRESOLVED, KIDS_MIGRATION_REVIEW — plus three purely informational history flags. Flags auto-clear when the underlying fact becomes true, or an admin clears them explicitly (logged).

Step 10 in depth

Admin review — the last human gate

Submitted products (status PENDING_REVIEW) land in the Super Admin’s review queue. The admin verifies everything, then chooses. Implemented

What the admin checks

✓ Product identity (permanent ID)
✓ Name (real, right department)
✓ Price (> 0, pricing engine clean)
✓ Taxonomy (category, subcategory)
✓ Grouping (no open identity question)
✓ Media (primary exists, views correct)
✓ Ownership (no conflicts)
✓ Inventory (Kids: stock or made-to-order)
✓ Required fields (SKU, description)
✓ Review flags (all blockers gone)

Then one of three outcomes

RETURN

Back to DRAFT with a written reason (stored as a rejection). The assigned employee continues work.

APPROVE

General products: approve and publish in one act (the button honestly says “Approve & Publish”). Kids products: approval is recorded first, publish is a separate second act.

PUBLISH

The final act — re-validates every blocker at the moment of publishing. Kids products additionally require the recorded approval.

Note (verified quirk): a separate approve-then-publish step for non-Kids products is Not Implemented — general approval publishes immediately.

Technical implementation

The Complete Journey

End-to-end: a kids image becomes a live product

A realistic walk-through using the real IDs from the repository — kids-021.webp → KID-021. Every stage is the actual implemented behavior.

1 · kids-021.webp — enters the library

The photograph lands in public/library, the single canonical media root, via the ingestion pipeline.

2 · Register

The system records it: a permanent Media ID, path, checksum, dimensions, and status. The image now has an identity.

3 · Classify

The filename parser reads kids-021: groupKey kids-021, no view word → a standalone plate (one photo, no hover alternate).

4 · Group

One file, one group. The confirmed Kids identity table says this plate maps to exactly one permanent Product ID — and may never merge with any other kids plate, however similar they look.

5 · KID-021 — draft is minted

The Kids draft migration creates product KID-021: status DRAFT, category kidswear, the plate claimed as primary. Customers cannot see it.

6 · Review flags raised

Anything the system could not prove is flagged: name review, price review, taxonomy review — and a conflict flag if the plate is currently owned by a different published product.

7 · Employee review

An admin assigns an active employee. The employee opens My Product Review, sees the mandatory media preview, types the real name (“Boys’ Festive Kurta Set”), sets the price, subcategory and stock, saves the draft, and presses Submit for Review.

8 · Admin review

KID-021 appears in the admin queue with the 9-item Kids checklist: correct media, name, category, subcategory, price, employee reviewed, admin reviewed, ready, published. Any remaining conflict is resolved with keep / transfer / merge / separate.

9 · Approval

The admin approves — the approval is recorded, but the product is still not live. Kids products require a separate, explicit publish.

10 · Publish

The admin publishes. The gate re-checks every blocker at this exact moment. Status becomes PUBLISHED; the publisher’s name and the time are stamped; the diary records it.

11 · /category/kidswear

The shared storefront list now includes KID-021. It appears in Explore (deduped by Product ID), the kids category page, and its own detail page at /product/kid-021.

12 · Customer

A shopper sees the card — the kids-021 plate, no hover swap (standalone) — opens the page, and adds it to the cart. The journey is complete.

Plain Language

Glossary

Every term in this guide, explained in one or two simple sentences.

Media
Any image or visual file the business uses — product photos and marketing artwork.
Asset
One individual media file, together with its recorded information.
Media ID
The permanent identity code of one media record (e.g. pm-lz3k9-0a4). It never changes.
Product ID
The permanent identity code of one product (e.g. KID-021, SAR-005). It is never derived from the product’s name.
GroupKey
The part of a filename before the view word (e.g. women-saree-001). All files sharing it photograph the same physical product.
View
The angle a photo shows: front, side, back, detail, close, left, right.
Primary
The main image of a product — the one every card and listing shows first.
Gallery
The ordered set of all images a product owns, shown on its detail page.
Product Mapping
The decision of which product (if any) a media asset belongs to.
Ownership
The product ID written on a media record — the single truth of who the photo belongs to.
Claim
A product saying “this media is mine”. If the media record disagrees, that is a conflict a human must resolve.
Taxonomy
The category tree of the shop: categories (Sarees, Kids Wear…) and their subcategories.
Review Flag
A sticky note on a draft saying a human must check something (name, price, taxonomy, grouping). Blocking flags stop publication.
Draft
A product that exists internally but is invisible to customers. Every new product starts here.
Published
The only product state customers can see. Reached only through review and approval.
Marketing Media
Promotional artwork (hero, banner, editorial) owned by a placement on the site — never by a product.
Media Resolver
The one system component that chooses which artwork each homepage/section slot shows, using fixed, repeatable rules.
Product Media Set
The assembled answer to “which images does this product provably own?” — primary, hover, gallery and named views.
Review Queue
The waiting rooms where uploads and identity questions sit until a human decides.
Product Workflow
The controlled pipeline every product travels: media → draft → employee review → admin review → approval → publish.
Placement
A named slot on the storefront that marketing media can occupy (e.g. HOME_HERO, EDITORIAL).
Standalone
A media file with no view word — one photo representing one product, with no hover alternate.
